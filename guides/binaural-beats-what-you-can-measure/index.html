Binaural beats: what you can and cannot measure

A binaural beat is a difference, not a frequency. That distinction decides what a spectrum can show and what only an EEG could.

What a binaural beat is

Send 200 Hz to the left ear and 204 Hz to the right. Each ear hears a steady tone; the auditory system perceives a slow pulse at the 4 Hz difference. The beat is a product of how the brain combines the two inputs, not a 4 Hz sound in the room.

This is why the track needs headphones: with speakers, both tones reach both ears and the effect is different.

What a spectrum shows

An FFT of the mix shows the two carriers — 200 and 204 Hz — as separate peaks, plus any modulation sidebands. It does not show a peak at 4 Hz, because no 4 Hz energy is being produced. If a track is generated by amplitude-modulating a single carrier, the spectrum instead shows the carrier plus sidebands at carrier ± beat frequency.

The workbench downmixes to mono for analysis, so both carriers are present in the signal it sees. That is useful: you can confirm both tones exist and measure their spacing.

The resolution problem

At 48 kHz the fine spectrum resolves about 1.46 Hz per bin. A 4 Hz beat is under three bins wide, and two peaks that close can merge into one broad peak, especially with a window that smears them. At low carriers — say 100 and 104 Hz — the two tones are only about 2.7 bins apart. Expect to see a widened peak rather than two clean lines.

What helps: a longer analysis window (not adjustable here), a higher carrier frequency (the bin spacing is constant, but the relative separation is smaller at high frequencies), and looking at the spectrogram over time for a repeating pulse in the carrier band.

How to check a binaural track

  1. Open the file, ideally with headphones so you hear what the track intends.
  2. Find the carrier in the peak table. Set the target to that frequency with a tight tolerance.
  3. Look for a second peak a few hertz away. If the two merge, note the widened band instead of inventing a number.
  4. Use the spectrogram: a steady carrier with a periodic thickening is consistent with amplitude modulation; two steady carriers look like two lines.
  5. Remember the difference: the claimed beat frequency is the spacing, not the carrier.

What cannot be measured here

Whether the beat is perceived. That depends on the listener, the headphones and the level. A spectrum cannot tell you what someone hears.

Whether brainwave entrainment happens. That requires EEG or another physiological measure, with controls. Claims that a track “moves you into theta” are not testable with an FFT of the audio.

The intended beat if the track is mono. A mono file cannot deliver different frequencies to each ear; if the upload is mono, the binaural mechanism is gone regardless of what the description says.

Practical test. Set the target to the carrier, play the file, and watch whether the band is steady. Then inspect the peak width. A single narrow line means one tone; two lines or a stable widened band means you are seeing the pair.
