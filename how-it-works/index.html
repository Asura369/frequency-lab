Method

How the measurement works

Enough detail to judge the numbers — and to know when not to trust them. Everything here is implemented in the page you can open; there is no hidden model.

The short version

Audio is captured or decoded, a 32,768-sample window is analysed roughly 60 times per second, the strongest peaks are found above a median noise floor, and a target band is checked for a tone-like peak. Evidence is counted over time instead of reported as a single frame.

Resolution and the analysis window

The fine spectrum uses an FFT of 32,768 samples. At 48 kHz that is a bin every 1.46 Hz and a window of about 0.68 s; at 44.1 kHz it is 1.35 Hz and 0.74 s. The browser applies a window function before the transform, so a steady tone appears as a peak a few bins wide, not a single line.

Two consequences follow. First, two tones closer than roughly 1.5 Hz merge into one peak. Second, anything that changes faster than about 0.7 s is smeared across the window. That is why the readout lags a fast vibrato and why the spectrogram is the better view for rhythm.

Dominant peak and the peak list

Each frame, the analyser returns dB per bin. The workbench:

  1. estimates the noise floor as the median of every eighth bin between 20 Hz and 20 kHz;
  2. keeps local maxima that are louder than the floor by 6 dB and above an absolute −100 dB level;
  3. measures salience — the peak level minus the median of its sidebands 4–32 bins away;
  4. rejects peaks with less than 6 dB of salience, suppresses neighbours within 8 bins, and keeps the six strongest;
  5. refines the centre with a parabolic fit over the three nearest bins, which recovers a fraction of a bin.

The headline number is the strongest peak, smoothed over five frames. The peak table stays instantaneous, so you can see it flicker when the source does.

Note and cents

The note is derived from equal temperament with A4 = 440 Hz: midi = 69 + 12·log₂(f / 440). Cents are (midi − nearest)·100, so 432 Hz reads as A4 −31.8 cents. Sharps are used throughout. This is a reference calculation, not a claim that the music is in that key.

Target evidence

For a target f and tolerance ±t:

  • Band SNR is the power-averaged level of the bins inside the band minus the noise floor.
  • Prominence is the loudest bin in the band minus the median of the surrounding sidebands.
  • A frame counts as tone present when band SNR ≥ 6 dB and prominence ≥ 6 dB.

Frames are grouped into 100 ms buckets. A bucket counts as present when at least half of its signal frames detect a tone. The percentages ignore silent buckets, so they answer “when there was signal, how often was the target there?” — not “how often was there sound?”.

Presence is not dominance. The target can be a quiet layer under a much louder peak — the card reports the strongest peak separately, plus how far the in-band target peak sits below it. A green verdict means the target exists, not that it is the loudest thing in the audio.

The verdict uses the session percentage: 80% or more is consistent, 20–80% is intermittent, below 20% is not detected. With fewer than ten signal buckets it reports collecting instead of guessing.

The signal gate

A session is considered live above roughly −70 dBFS RMS and goes quiet below −75 dBFS, with hysteresis (3 frames on, 8 frames off). The dB values are relative to full scale, not calibrated sound pressure. Microphone processing — echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain — is disabled, but the microphone’s own sensitivity is unknown, so do not read dB as SPL.

The spectrogram

The waterfall uses a separate 8,192-sample FFT, one column every 40 ms, 20 Hz–20 kHz on a log axis, and colour mapped from −100 to −30 dB. The window shown in the interface is the time span of the visible texture (roughly 20–40 s depending on width). Pausing freezes the columns; the analysis and audio keep running.

Whole-file analysis

“Analyse file” decodes the whole file with the browser’s decoder, mixes it to mono and runs a 16,384-point FFT in a worker — about 1.46 Hz per bin at 48 kHz and a 0.34 s window. Frames are taken every 2,048 samples for short files and wider hops for long ones, capped at 4,000 frames, so a ten-minute file finishes in seconds rather than real time.

Every frame yields the dominant peak, the noise floor and the same target evidence used live. The report adds the share of signal time the target is present, six band-energy shares, ten equal sections, recurring peaks, and a downsampled spectrogram of the whole file. It is exported as JSON, per-frame CSV or a PNG.

Limits: 10 minutes or 200 MB per file, mono downmix, no streaming decode and no absolute calibration. The live workbench remains the path for longer files and for microphone or tab capture.

What it cannot tell you

  • The pitch of a chord or a mix. A spectrum peak is a partial, not a fundamental. See the guide.
  • Sound pressure level or “dose”. No calibration, no weighting curve, no dosimeter maths.
  • Whether an effect is real. A tone at 528 Hz is a tone at 528 Hz; the workbench stops there.
  • Anything below 20 Hz. The analysis floor is 20 Hz, and laptop microphones roll off well before 7.83 Hz.
  • Sub-bin truth. Interpolation improves a clean peak; it does not create resolution that the window does not have.

How the tool checks itself

“Run self-test” drives an internal oscillator at 440, 432 and 528 Hz and asserts a reading within ±0.2 Hz. The same test runs in the automated browser suite. It exercises the audio graph, both analysers and the readout without needing a microphone.
